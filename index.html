    If you were just told you have this

    Take a breath. You've found the right place.

    If you're reading this, someone probably just said the words "papillary tumor of the pineal region" to you, and then the internet gave you almost nothing. That's not because the news is bad. It's because this tumour is so rare that only a few hundred people have ever been written about since it was first named in 2003. Here is what is actually known, gathered in one calm place.

    PTPR grows deep in the centre of the brain, beside the pineal gland. It is thought to start from a tiny structure called the subcommissural organ, not the pineal gland itself. Most people first notice it because of headaches: the tumour sits where fluid drains inside the brain, and when that drain is squeezed, pressure builds (hydrocephalus). The World Health Organization classes it as CNS WHO grade 2 or 3: a real tumour that needs treatment and long follow-up, but not among the most aggressive brain tumours.

    Everything here comes from the published medical literature, with the source shown next to each claim, and it is written for patients and families rather than for doctors. It is not a substitute for your own medical team, and it was made by someone who loves a person with PTPR, not by a hospital.

    The questions everyone asks

    Honest answers, with the numbers behind them

    These are the questions people type into search engines at 2 a.m. The numbers come from the largest pooled studies, but please hold them loosely: they describe small groups of patients, many treated 10 to 20 years ago, before molecular testing and modern radiotherapy. They cannot predict one person's path.

    How long do people live with this?

    73% of patients were alive five years after diagnosis in the first multicentre series (31 patients, 2006), and the same study's authors later followed 44 patients, 32 of whom were alive after a median of more than five years. A 2019 analysis pooling every published case found 83.5% alive at three years.

    The most recent pooled analysis (108 patients, 2024) found 87.5% alive at five years and 80.2% at ten. Behind those averages are individual published stories: people with no progression more than 7, 9 and 10 years after diagnosis, and one person alive 18 years on despite several recurrences.

    What the numbers can't tell you: they describe patients diagnosed mostly between 2003 and 2017. Methylation profiling, proton therapy and targeted drugs barely existed then. Nobody can tell you your number, and that uncertainty cuts both ways.

    Sources: Fèvre-Montange 2006 · Fauchon 2013 · Yamaki 2019 · Pang 2024 · individual stories are in the case registry

    Does it come back?

    Often, yes, and this is the single most important thing to understand about PTPR. In the pooled analysis, 57% of patients had a recurrence, at a median of 29 months. In the 2024 molecular series, 14 of the 31 patients with follow-up had progressed, and the median time without progression was 63 months. Recurrence is almost always local, in the same place, and sometimes appears years later. That is why your team will keep scanning you for a long time.

    A recurrence is not a death sentence. The literature is full of people treated again, with a second operation, radiosurgery, radiotherapy or, more recently, targeted drugs, and then stable for years. Being followed closely is the point: it catches regrowth when it is small.

    Sources: Yamaki 2019 · Wu 2024

    Do people die from it?

    Some do. In a review of 73 published patients, 3 had died at the time their reports were written, and 50 had no sign of recurrence. Deaths in the literature cluster around repeated recurrences, tumours that could not be fully removed, and surgical complications in the earliest years. Most people in published reports were alive at their last follow-up.

    The overall picture is of a tumour people live with, under surveillance, more often than one they die from. Several doctors who have written about PTPR describe it as unpredictable rather than aggressive.

    Source: Nasrollahi 2016

    Is it cancer?

    It sits in between. CNS WHO grade 2 or 3 means it is not benign (grade 1), because it can regrow and, rarely, spread through the spinal fluid, but it is not grade 4 like glioblastoma. Doctors treat it seriously and follow it for years for exactly that reason. The grade itself is less settled than for other tumours: when the WHO first listed PTPR it noted that precise grading criteria "remain to be defined".

    Sources: WHO 2007 summary · WHO CNS5, 2021

    Does it spread?

    Usually it stays where it is. Spread through the cerebrospinal fluid to the spine or the lining of the brain (leptomeningeal seeding) has been reported in a minority of cases, which is why an MRI of the whole spine is part of the standard work-up at diagnosis and sometimes during follow-up.

    Sources: Kim 2010 · Nowicka 2016

    If it was completely removed, am I done?

    In the largest multicentre study (44 patients), complete removal (gross total resection) was linked to longer survival, although a later pooled analysis did not find a clear extra benefit over partial removal. There are published patients who had their tumour fully removed, no further treatment, and no recurrence after nearly five years. But even after complete removal, recurrence happens: around 39% of children in one review, and in the multicentre study complete removal did not lengthen the time before the tumour came back. So "done" means "done with this chapter": follow-up scans continue, often yearly, for a decade or more.

    Sources: Fauchon 2013 · Yamaki 2019 · Cañizares-Méndez 2019 · Balci Topuz 2026

    Did I do something to cause this?

    No. There are no known lifestyle, environmental or dietary risk factors for PTPR. It is not caused by stress, phones, diet or anything you did or didn't do. In almost every case it is not inherited either; a handful of recent reports describe inherited changes in a gene called PTEN in people with PTPR, which is a research lead, not a reason for most families to worry.

    Sources: Royal-Preyra 2025 · O'Neal 2025

    Should I get a second opinion?

    For a tumour this rare, almost every expert would say yes, at least for the pathology. PTPR is regularly mistaken for other papillary tumours; several published patients carried a wrong diagnosis for years. Ask whether DNA methylation profiling was done, and whether your slides can be reviewed at a centre that sees pineal tumours. Good doctors are not offended by this.

    What usually happens next

    Care differs between countries and hospitals, and there is no single agreed protocol because the tumour is so rare. These are the steps that appear again and again in the published cases.

    1. Relieving the pressureIf hydrocephalus is present, the first operation is often a small one to let fluid drain: an endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) or a shunt. Headaches, nausea and vision problems often ease quickly.
    2. Finding out exactly what it isMRI of the brain and whole spine, blood and spinal-fluid tests for germ-cell tumour markers (AFP and beta-hCG), sometimes spinal-fluid cytology. A definite diagnosis of PTPR needs tissue.
    3. SurgeryRemoving as much tumour as can be done safely is the step most consistently linked to better outcomes. This is deep, delicate surgery; ask whether your surgeon or centre has experience with pineal-region tumours.
    4. Pathology, including molecular testingUnder the microscope PTPR resembles several other tumours. Cytokeratin staining and, increasingly, DNA methylation profiling confirm the diagnosis and can place it in subgroup PTPR-A or PTPR-B.
    5. The radiotherapy conversationMany centres offer radiotherapy after surgery, especially when tumour is left behind, but retrospective studies disagree on how much it helps. This is a reasonable moment for a second opinion from a neuro-oncology centre.
    6. Long-term follow-upPTPR tends to come back locally, sometimes years later. Regular MRI for many years is standard. Many people settle into a rhythm of yearly scans and otherwise ordinary lives.

    Questions worth asking your team

    Has the diagnosis been confirmed by a neuropathologist with pineal-tumour experience?
    PTPR is often mistaken for papillary ependymoma, choroid plexus tumours or metastases. A second pathology read at a large centre is common and reasonable.
    Was DNA methylation profiling done? Which subgroup is it?
    Methylation profiling is now the most reliable way to confirm PTPR and distinguishes PTPR-A from PTPR-B. Published series suggest group B may recur sooner, which can affect how closely you are followed.
    How much of the tumour was removed, and what does the post-operative MRI show?
    Knowing whether residual tumour is present shapes the radiotherapy discussion.
    What is the plan for follow-up imaging, and for how long?
    Because late recurrence is documented, many teams continue MRI surveillance for a decade or more.
    Is there a rare-tumour programme or natural-history study I can join?
    In North America, the NCI-CONNECT programme studies rare central nervous system tumours and offers consultations. In Europe, EURACAN is the reference network for rare adult cancers.

    Words you will hear

    Pineal region
    The very centre of the brain, around the pineal gland, which makes melatonin. Tumours here block the narrow channel that drains fluid from the brain.
    Hydrocephalus
    Build-up of cerebrospinal fluid because its drainage is blocked. Causes headache, nausea, sleepiness and vision changes. Treated with an ETV or a shunt.
    ETV
    Endoscopic third ventriculostomy: a small operation that makes a new opening for fluid to drain, avoiding a permanent shunt.
    Parinaud's syndrome
    Difficulty looking upward, with pupil changes, from pressure on the top of the brainstem. Reported in a minority of PTPR patients.
    Gross total resection
    Surgical removal of all visible tumour. Subtotal resection means some was left behind, often deliberately to protect vital structures.
    Methylation profiling
    A laboratory test that reads chemical marks on the tumour's DNA and compares them with thousands of known tumours. Very accurate for PTPR.
    PTPR-A / PTPR-B
    The two molecular subgroups of PTPR described in 2016 and confirmed in 2024.
    CNS WHO grade
    The World Health Organization's 1-to-4 scale of how a brain tumour is expected to behave. PTPR is grade 2 or 3.
    Subcommissural organ
    A tiny gland-like structure next to the pineal gland, present in the developing brain, from which PTPR is thought to arise.

    Why this exists

    A gift, and a promise

    In 2016, someone I had not yet met was diagnosed with a brain tumour so rare that the doctors themselves had to look it up. Then came the scans, the surgery and the long silence afterwards, in which you wait for a machine to tell you whether you can keep making plans.

    Years later, I met him, and he told me about it. I am an academic, and research is how I make sense of the world, so I did what I know how to do: I started reading the articles that had been published about this tumour. I needed data to feel secure in this new situation. What I found was a handful of case reports, a few paragraphs of statistics, and very little written for the people actually living with it.

    That reading is how this website began. I wanted to bring what I found together in one place, so that the next person who receives this diagnosis, and the people who love them, will not have to search in the dark the way we did. It is a gift to him and a promise to them. Everything I could find is here, in plain language, with the sources shown, and with room for your story next to ours.

    He is doing well. His scans have been stable for years now, and life has settled into yearly MRIs and everything that happens in between them. That is not a guarantee for anyone else, but it is a true story, and true stories were what we both wished we'd had.

    My thanks go to his parents and his two sisters, who held him up long before I arrived, to his friends, and to the staff at Sunnybrook who have looked after him from the first frightening weeks through every scan since. This site exists because of all of you.

    — Moni, Ottawa

      How many people have had PTPR?

      Nobody knows the true number. There is no registry anywhere in the world, and most people who are diagnosed are never written about. What can be counted is the medical literature: from time to time doctors publish a review that counts the cases reported so far. The largest of those counts are plotted below.       You are not alone

      Support

      With so few people diagnosed, there is no PTPR-only charity or support group anywhere in the world yet. The organisations below support people with any rare brain tumour, and several have mentor programmes that will try to match you with someone whose situation is close to yours.

      Find each other

      This site wants to become the place where people with PTPR, their families and the people who have lost someone can meet. If you would like to be put in touch with others, or to help start a small private group (email list, WhatsApp, Discord or a monthly video call), write to . Nothing is published or shared without your written agreement.

      If you want to start a group yourself

      1. Pick one private channel people already use (a WhatsApp or Signal group, or a private Facebook group).
      2. Ask RareConnect (EURORDIS) to host a moderated PTPR community; they do this free for rare diseases.
      3. Agree on three rules early: no medical advice, no selling, privacy by default.
      4. Tell this site, so the group can be listed here for the next person who is diagnosed.

      Accuracy and the numbers

      Every medical statement links to the peer-reviewed article it comes from, and the statistics, case details and summaries have been checked against those articles by a person. Even so, mistakes are possible: the site is maintained by a volunteer, the underlying research is based on very small numbers of patients, and knowledge about PTPR changes. Survival and recurrence figures describe groups of past patients, not any individual, and they must not be read as a prediction for you or anyone you love. Please read the original articles yourself, draw your own conclusions with your medical team, and tell us if you find an error so that it can be corrected.

      The case registry summarises patients described in published reports. It is not a medical registry, it is not complete, and it must not be used for research or clinical decisions without going back to the original publications.
