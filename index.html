If you were just told you have this

Take a breath. Here is what is known.

A papillary tumor of the pineal region (PTPR) is one of the rarest brain tumours there is. Only a few hundred cases have ever been described in the medical literature since it was first named in 2003. That rarity is why your search results are so thin, why even some doctors have never seen one, and why this page exists.

PTPR grows deep in the centre of the brain, next to the pineal gland, and it is thought to start from a tiny structure called the subcommissural organ rather than from the pineal gland itself. Most people first notice it because of headaches: the tumour sits where the fluid inside the brain drains, and when that drain is blocked, pressure builds (hydrocephalus). It is classified by the World Health Organization as CNS WHO grade 2 or 3, which means it is a real tumour that needs treatment and long follow-up, but it is not among the most aggressive brain tumours.

Everything on this site is drawn from the published medical literature, with the source shown next to each claim. It is written for patients and families, not for doctors, and it is not a substitute for your own medical team.

What usually happens next

Care differs between countries and hospitals, and there is no single agreed protocol because the tumour is so rare. These are the steps that appear again and again in the published cases.

  1. Relieving the pressureIf hydrocephalus is present, the first operation is often a small one to let fluid drain: an endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) or a shunt. Headaches, nausea and vision problems often improve quickly after this.
  2. Finding out exactly what it isMRI of the brain and whole spine, blood and spinal-fluid tests for germ-cell tumour markers (AFP and beta-hCG), and sometimes spinal-fluid cytology. These rule out the more common pineal-region tumours. A definite diagnosis of PTPR needs tissue.
  3. SurgeryRemoving as much of the tumour as can be done safely is the step most consistently linked to better outcomes in the literature. This is deep, delicate surgery; ask whether your surgeon or centre has experience with pineal-region tumours.
  4. Pathology, including molecular testingUnder the microscope PTPR can look like several other tumours. Immunohistochemistry (cytokeratin positive) and, increasingly, DNA methylation profiling confirm the diagnosis and can place the tumour in subgroup PTPR-A or PTPR-B. Ask whether methylation profiling has been done or can be requested.
  5. Discussion of radiotherapyMany centres offer radiotherapy after surgery, especially when tumour is left behind, but retrospective studies disagree on how much it helps. This is an area where a second opinion from a neuro-oncology centre is reasonable.
  6. Long-term follow-upPTPR tends to come back locally, sometimes years later (median about 28 months in one pooled analysis; some recurrences after 5 years). Regular MRI for many years is standard. A recurrence is not the end of the road: many people have been treated again with surgery, radiosurgery or radiotherapy.

Questions worth asking your team

Has the diagnosis been confirmed by a neuropathologist with pineal-tumour experience?
PTPR is often mistaken for papillary ependymoma, choroid plexus tumours or metastases. A second pathology read at a large centre is common and reasonable.
Was DNA methylation profiling done? Which subgroup is it?
Methylation profiling is now the most reliable way to confirm PTPR and distinguishes PTPR-A from PTPR-B. Published series suggest group B may recur sooner, so it can affect how closely you are followed.
How much of the tumour was removed, and what does the post-operative MRI show?
Gross total resection was linked to longer survival in the largest multicentre study. Knowing whether residual tumour is present shapes the radiotherapy discussion.
What is the plan for follow-up imaging, and for how long?
Because late recurrence is documented, many teams continue MRI surveillance for a decade or more.
Is there a rare-tumour programme or natural-history study I can join?
In North America, the US National Cancer Institute's NCI-CONNECT programme studies rare central nervous system tumours and offers consultations. Similar rare-tumour networks exist in Europe (EURACAN).
Could I speak with someone else who has had this?
There is no PTPR-specific patient organisation yet. The Support section lists brain-tumour charities with mentor programmes, and this site is building a way for people with PTPR to find one another.

Words you will hear

Pineal region
The very centre of the brain, around the pineal gland, which makes melatonin. Tumours here block the narrow channel that drains fluid from the brain.
Hydrocephalus
Build-up of cerebrospinal fluid because its drainage is blocked. Causes headache, nausea, sleepiness and vision changes. Treated with an ETV or a shunt.
ETV
Endoscopic third ventriculostomy: a small operation that makes a new opening for fluid to drain, avoiding a permanent shunt.
Parinaud's syndrome
Difficulty looking upward, with pupil changes, caused by pressure on the top of the brainstem. Reported in a minority of PTPR patients.
Gross total resection
Surgical removal of all visible tumour. Subtotal resection means some was left behind, often deliberately to protect vital structures.
Methylation profiling
A laboratory test that reads chemical marks on the tumour's DNA and compares them with thousands of known tumours. Very accurate for PTPR.
PTPR-A / PTPR-B
The two molecular subgroups of PTPR described in 2016 and confirmed in 2024.
CNS WHO grade
The World Health Organization's 1-to-4 scale of how a brain tumour is expected to behave. PTPR is grade 2 or 3.
Subcommissural organ
A tiny gland-like structure next to the pineal gland, present in the developing brain, from which PTPR is thought to arise.

    Support

    With so few people diagnosed, there is no PTPR-only charity or support group anywhere in the world yet. The organisations below support people with any rare brain tumour, and several have mentor programmes that will try to match you with someone whose situation is close to yours.

    Find each other

    If you want to start a group yourself

    1. Pick one private channel people already use (a WhatsApp or Signal group, or a private Facebook group).
    2. Ask RareConnect (EURORDIS) to host a moderated PTPR community; they do this for free for rare diseases.
    3. Agree on three rules early: no medical advice, no selling, and privacy by default.
    4. Tell this site, so the group can be listed here for the next person who is diagnosed.
